(~2 min) Thesis: Taiwan matters far beyond its size because it sits at the chokepoint of the electronics supply chain, and it is tied most closely to four partners. ASEAN: Taiwanese firms operate factories in Vietnam, Thailand, Malaysia and Indonesia; migrant workers fill labour gaps; the New Southbound Policy (2016) aims to deepen these ties and diversify away from China. Q2 · CAPITALISM, GLOBALIZATION & FREE TRADE

Globalization: growth engine and vulnerability

Gains

  • GDP per capita from ~US$1,000 (1970s) to >US$30,000
  • Export-led growth with relatively low inequality
  • US aid, technology and markets enabled early take-off
  • Global value-chain niche: chips, ICT, machinery

Costs & risks

  • Hollowing out: factories moved to China after 1987
  • Wage stagnation; dual economy: high-wage chip giants vs low-productivity small firms and services
  • Dependence on one sector and on US–China relations
  • Environmental pressure: energy, water, land
  • Ageing and very low fertility (TFR ≈ 0.9)
(~1.5 min) Be balanced: past and present. Past: US aid and the open US market in the 1950s-70s; export processing zones (Kaohsiung, 1966); later the PC/ICT boom. Costs: after the 1987 currency appreciation and the lifting of capital controls, labour-intensive industry relocated to China and Southeast Asia, hollowing out parts of the domestic base. Dual economy: a split between high-wage, high-productivity chip and electronics giants and lower-productivity small firms and services, so overall growth does not lift the typical worker's pay. Today the risks are concentration in one sector, geopolitical exposure, the power and water demands of fabs, and demographic decline. Free trade benefited Taiwan only because the state shaped it (selective protection, industrial policy) - not pure laissez-faire.

(~2 min) Walk the timeline. The pattern: each external shock pushed upgrading rather than collapse. 1970s oil shocks led to the Ten Major Construction projects and heavy industry. 1985-87 currency appreciation and liberalisation led to factories relocating to China and Southeast Asia, while the state doubled down on Hsinchu and ITRI. 1990s: Taiwan built PCs and chips for other brands, and the foundry model emerged; Taiwan weathered the 1997 Asian crisis thanks to low foreign debt and flexible small firms. 2000s: 'design in Taiwan, make in China'; hit by the 2001 dot-com crash (tech spending collapsed, GDP fell) and the 2008-09 global financial crisis (exports fell about 20% in 2009). 2010s-now: smartphones, AI chips, and reshoring to the US/Japan/Europe plus New Southbound diversification. Q3 · HAS THE ECONOMY MOVED ONTO A NEW GROWTH PATH?

Taiwan has a new growth path, but a narrow one

Yes: a real new path

  • Structural shift. From farming and cheap labour-intensive goods to high-tech chips and electronics.
  • Higher output. Output per worker and incomes rose sharply; TSMC and its suppliers now drive growth and exports.
  • Resilient. Taiwan bounced back from big shocks (oil crises, a stronger currency, the 2001 and 2008 downturns) and kept growing, so the change is lasting.

But narrow

  • One sector. Chips and electronics are a large share of exports and growth; a sector downturn or US–China conflict would hit everything.
  • Few firms. A handful of big chip firms capture most of the gains; most workers are at small firms where pay and productivity lag. This split is called a dual economy.
  • Few regions. Growth clusters in the Hsinchu–Taipei–Taichung corridor; rural and southern areas lag.
  • Few new industries. Biotech, green energy and AI services are still small next to chips.
(~1 min) Direct answer to Q3. Yes: Taiwan has moved from agriculture and cheap labour-intensive goods to high-tech chips and electronics; output per worker and incomes rose sharply; the shift survived several crises, so it is a structural change, not a one-off. But it is narrow: one sector carries the economy (exposed to a chip downturn or US-China conflict); a handful of big chip firms capture most of the gains while most workers are at small firms where pay and productivity lag (the dual economy); growth concentrates in the Hsinchu-Taipei-Taichung corridor; and other new industries such as biotech, green energy and AI services are still small compared with chips.

Q4 · POLICY AND LOCAL IMPACT

Regional policy: national, top-down, uneven

Industrial & spatial

Export processing zones (1966); Hsinchu (1980), Southern (1996) and Central (2003) Science Parks; Ten Major Construction

Impact: Created high-tech clusters; strong north–west corridor

Regional revitalization

Local Revitalization (2019): tackle depopulation, support local brands & youth return

Impact: Early stage; mixed results

Result: growth poles in Hsinchu, Taipei and Taichung, but a persistent north–south and urban–rural gap.

(~1.5 min) Taiwan has no EU-style regional policy; instead a developmental state used spatial policy as industrial policy. Science parks combine land, infrastructure, tax incentives and links to universities and the state research institute. Local Revitalization (2019, led by the National Development Council, the central planning agency) responds to depopulation and ageing outside metropolitan areas. Impact: strong clusters (Hsinchu semiconductors, Taichung machinery, Tainan/Kaohsiung heavy industry and new green energy) but regional inequality persists. PART 2 · ASSESSING DEVELOPMENT OUTCOMES

Assessing outcomes: who drove new paths

1 · New paths pursued?

Yes: electronics (1970s–80s), then foundry/fabless chips (1987→), now AI & green energy. Largely successful.

2 · Who led?

National government (state research institute, central planning agency) – no EU-style body; local councils secondary.

3 · What sparked it?

Hsinchu Science Park + spin-offs from the state research institute (first chip maker UMC 1980, TSMC 1987); returnee engineers from Silicon Valley.

4 · Inside or outside?

Both: state-led from within, with US technology and markets and Taiwanese talent and money from abroad.

5 · Endogenous role

Family-run small businesses, 'living-room factories', clusters (Taichung machine tools), community action, local governments.

6 · Market forces

Global: US ICT demand, supply-chain shifts. National: credit, tax, R&D. Local: cluster networks.

(~2 min) This slide answers the six assessment questions in order. 1) New paths: yes - and successful in chips. 2) Lead actor: the national government, through the state research institute (ITRI) and science-park agencies; there is no EU-type body. 3) Spark: deliberate state creation of Hsinchu plus technology transfer (the 1976 RCA licence) and spin-offs; TSMC founded 1987 with state-backed capital and Philips. 4) Mixed: inside-led (state) but dependent on outside demand, technology and returnee talent. 5) Endogenous: dense networks of family-run firms, cluster governance, local government and community initiatives - strongest in manufacturing clusters, weaker in peripheral regions. 6) Market forces operate at three scales. CONCLUSION

Conclusion: a hybrid development model

Inherited economic systems

Japanese-era infrastructure and land reform; capitalism based on small and medium-sized family firms; state-owned enterprises.

Influence: Strong

Long-standing social strengths

High education, entrepreneurial networks, returnees from abroad, strong civic and community life.

Influence: Strong

External support

US aid (1951–65), markets and technology; Japanese tools and materials; worldwide demand for computers, phones and chips.

Influence: Decisive early, conditional now

(~1.5 min) Synthesis. No single factor explains Taiwan's outcomes: inherited institutions (land reform, small-firm capitalism, a capable state) set the base; long-standing social strengths (education, networks, returnees) supplied the capability; external support (US aid 1951-65, US markets and technology, Japanese tools and materials, worldwide demand for computers, phones and chips) opened the door. 'Hybrid' means a mix of these: state-led planning, private firms, local initiative and outside help working together.

CONCLUSION

Takeaway: state-guided and externally enabled

State-guided: the government led

  • Hsinchu Science Park (1980): built and subsidised by the government
  • ITRI research institute (1973): developed technology and spun off UMC and TSMC
  • TSMC (1987): a government fund was its largest shareholder at the start
  • Export zones, tax breaks and the 1970s Ten Major Construction projects

Externally enabled: outside help and demand

  • US aid (1951–65) and open access to the US market
  • Technology transfer: chip-making know-how licensed from US firm Radio Corporation of America (RCA) in 1976; Philips as TSMC partner
  • Returnee engineers from Silicon Valley
  • Japanese chip-making machines, silicon wafers and chemicals; worldwide demand for computers, phones and chips

Then rooted locally: family-run firms and clusters made it last. Next challenge: diversify beyond chips and spread growth beyond the core cities.

(~1 min) Closing takeaway. STATE-GUIDED examples: Hsinchu Science Park (1980) was a government-built and subsidised site; ITRI (1973) developed chip technology and spun off UMC (1980) and TSMC (1987); at TSMC's founding the government's National Development Fund was the largest shareholder; export processing zones, tax incentives and the Ten Major Construction projects built the base. EXTERNALLY ENABLED examples: US aid 1951-65 and open access to the US market; technology transfer such as the 1976 RCA chip-technology licence and Philips as TSMC's partner; returnee engineers from Silicon Valley; Japanese chip-making machines, silicon wafers and chemicals; worldwide demand for computers, phones and chips. Locally rooted: family-run firms and clusters turned this into lasting growth. Next challenge: diversify beyond chips and spread growth beyond core cities.
